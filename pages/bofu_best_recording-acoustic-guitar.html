What Makes an Acoustic Record Well

A guitar that wows in a shop can disappoint in a mix. Recording rewards balance, clarity, and tuning stability over sheer loudness — a boomy dreadnought that fills a room can swallow a mix. Here's what matters behind the mic.

Balanced, controlled low end

Tight, even bass sits in a mix; excessive boom muddies it and eats up space your vocals and bass need. Smaller bodies (OM, grand auditorium) and mahogany often record cleaner than a big, bass-heavy dreadnought.

Note clarity and separation

Microphones expose everything, so you want articulate note definition and a top that responds evenly across the strings. This is where a well-built solid top and quality bracing earn their keep.

Mic it, don't rely on the pickup

For serious recording, a condenser microphone captures far more detail and natural tone than an onboard piezo pickup, which tends to sound thin and 'quacky.' The pickup is for live use; the mic is for the studio.

Tuning stability and fresh strings

Microphones reveal the slightest tuning drift, and dull strings sound dead on tape. Good tuners, a properly cut nut, and a fresh set of strings (let them stretch and settle first) make the biggest difference to a clean take.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a dreadnought good for recording?

It can be, but its strong low end often needs taming in a mix. Many engineers prefer smaller-bodied guitars (OM, grand auditorium) or mahogany dreadnoughts for recording because their tighter, more balanced response sits more easily alongside vocals and other instruments.

Should I record with a microphone or the built-in pickup?

Use a microphone for anything serious. Onboard piezo pickups are designed to fight feedback on a loud stage, not to capture natural tone, so they sound thin and synthetic on a recording. A single condenser mic positioned around the 12th–14th fret will outperform a pickup every time.

Do new strings really matter for recording?

Hugely. Old strings sound dull and lifeless on tape, where there's nothing to hide behind. Put on a fresh set, then play and re-tune for 20–30 minutes so they stop stretching before you hit record — bright, stable strings are free studio polish.
