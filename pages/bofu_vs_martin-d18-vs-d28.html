Martin D-18 vs D-28: Mahogany or Rosewood?

These two dreadnoughts are the heart of the classic 'mahogany vs rosewood' debate. Both are benchmark Martins; the right one comes down to the voice you want, not which is better.

Tonewood and voice

The D-18's mahogany back and sides give a dry, focused, punchy tone with strong midrange — great for flatpicking and cutting through a mix. The D-28's rosewood delivers deep lows and sparkling, scooped highs with more overtone complexity.

Best playing styles

The D-18 suits bluegrass flatpickers, singer-songwriters, and anyone who wants notes to stay defined. The D-28 favours big strummers and players who love a rich, piano-like spread of sound — it's the classic bluegrass-rhythm and recording dreadnought.

Feel and price

Both share Martin's premium build and similar necks, so the decision is overwhelmingly tonal. The D-28 typically costs a bit more thanks to rosewood; the D-18 is often the lighter, more immediate-feeling guitar.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the main difference between a Martin D-18 and D-28?

The back and sides: the D-18 uses mahogany for a dry, focused, midrange-forward tone, while the D-28 uses rosewood for deeper bass, sparkling highs, and more overtones. Both have a solid Sitka spruce top and Martin's dreadnought build — the wood is what sets their voices apart.

Is the D-28 worth the extra money over the D-18?

Only if you want its sound. The D-28's rosewood gives a richer, more complex, scooped tone that many strummers and recording players prefer. If you want a punchy, focused, mid-rich voice for flatpicking and vocal accompaniment, the D-18 is arguably the better — and often cheaper — choice.

Which is better for bluegrass?

Both are bluegrass staples. Flatpickers who want clear, articulate single-note runs often prefer the D-18's focused mahogany punch, while rhythm players who want a big, booming backdrop lean toward the D-28's rosewood depth. It comes down to your role in the band.
