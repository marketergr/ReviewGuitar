What to Look For in a Metal Guitar Under $800

Metal asks specific things of a guitar: tight high-gain clarity, a fast neck, and rock-solid tuning under aggressive playing and low tunings. Here's what separates a true metal machine from a general-purpose electric.

High-output pickups

Hot humbuckers — passive or active — give the tight, saturated attack metal needs and stay clear under heavy distortion where single-coils get fizzy and noisy. Active pickups offer an even more compressed, consistent high-gain tone favoured by many metal players.

Fast, thin neck

A flatter fretboard radius and a thin neck profile make fast runs, wide stretches, and shredding far more comfortable. This is a defining feature of 'superstrat' style metal guitars.

Bridge: fixed vs. Floyd Rose

A fixed or hardtail bridge holds tuning rock-solid and is simple to restring — ideal if you switch tunings. A Floyd Rose floating tremolo enables dramatic dive-bombs and flutter but is fussier to set up and retune. Choose based on whether you need the whammy theatrics.

Scale length for low tunings

If you drop-tune, a longer scale (25.5" or a baritone/multi-scale) keeps low strings tight and defined instead of floppy and muddy. For standard or one-step-down tuning, a normal scale is fine.

Frequently Asked Questions

Active or passive pickups for metal?

Both work; it's a tone choice. Active pickups (powered by a 9V battery) give a tight, compressed, consistent high-gain sound with very low noise — a metal staple. Passive high-output humbuckers sound a touch more dynamic and 'organic.' Under $800 you'll find excellent examples of each; pick by the tone you hear in your favourite bands.

Do I need a Floyd Rose tremolo for metal?

Only if you want dive-bombs and extreme whammy effects. A Floyd Rose locks tuning superbly once set up but is more complex to restring and retune. Many metal players prefer a fixed bridge for rock-solid tuning and easy tuning changes — it comes down to whether the whammy is part of your style.

What scale length is best for drop tunings?

For drop and low tunings, a longer scale length (25.5" or longer, up to baritone) keeps the thick low strings tight and articulate rather than loose and muddy. If you mostly play in standard or drop-D, a standard scale is perfectly fine; go longer the lower you tune.
